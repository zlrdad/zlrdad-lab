01 — The Diagnosis · Public Markets

Three boards.
Three different worlds.

Canada lists companies on three exchanges — the senior TSX, the TSX Venture board, and the CSE. Up close they are not one market at different sizes; they are three different animals. Understanding the difference is the first step to seeing why the public-market pathway has thinned — and why the fix is not cheaper listings.

— The missing middle

The barbell — and the gap where champions belong.

The TSX is a barbell: a cluster of mega-caps at one end, a long small-cap tail at the other, and a thin C$2–10B mid-cap waist in between. That waist is exactly where Canada's private champions would land if they listed at home.

— The read

The constraint is quality and liquidity — not listing cost.

The junior boards are not short of listings; they are short of quality issuers and trading. Canada actually has the highest median free float in the OECD's growth-market study (82%) — the shares are available to trade, but under-traded and under-covered. The lever, then, is market-making and research coverage (and a retail/again-domestic demand base), not cheaper admission.

Which raises the next question: if the public on-ramp has narrowed, where is the capital that would scale these companies — and who is providing it?

02 — The Diagnosis · The Financing Gap

Canada under-invests
in its own companies —
and foreign capital fills the gap.

If the public on-ramp has narrowed, the private one is where champions are actually built. But Canada deploys far less venture capital — relative to the size of its economy — than its peers, and the gap is sharpest exactly where it matters most: the scale-up rounds. What's missing gets supplied by US investors.

— The read

A multi-billion-dollar, stage-specific supply gap.

Canada's venture under-investment is real, large (~C$5–11B/yr), and concentrated at the scale-up stage — and what's missing is supplied by US capital that ultimately pulls control and upside south. The lever is to mobilise domestic growth capital and crowd it in, not to compel it.

But a gap is an abstraction. To see what's actually at stake, look at the companies themselves.

— What the roster teaches

Four patterns that decide the outcome.

Exit is by acquisition
The credible champions leave chiefly by being bought (Tenstorrent, Element AI, Untether) or taken private (Magnet) — at the growth/public stage, by US buyers.
Foreign control = exit by another name
Fullscript and PointClickCare keep Canadian HQs but are US-PE-controlled. Control and upside leave before the jobs do — so intervene earlier.
Retention is achievable
StackAdapt, Wealthsimple, Neo, bootstrapped Geotab/Jane, repatriated MDA — domestic capital and control do anchor champions. They're just rare.
Control cuts both ways
Dual-class let Shopify stay independent — and let Magnet's controllers sell to US PE. Governance preserves the option; it doesn't compel staying.
— A caveat worth naming

Canada's one public-market success is, so far, one family.

The serial-acquirer model is the standout — but Topicus and Lumine are controlled spin-offs of Constellation, not independent successes. The public-market win is concentrated in a single lineage (Constellation → Topicus → Lumine), with Descartes the one unrelated parallel. The model is replicable; the ecosystem hasn't yet replicated it.

— The read

The companies confirm the diagnosis.

Stays are possible but exceptional; the default trajectory is acquisition or a US listing, and foreign growth capital is the mechanism. If retention is achievable when domestic capital and control align, the question becomes: what did the jurisdictions that built and kept champions actually do?

04 — What Works Abroad

What the jurisdictions that
kept their champions did.

Canada's problem — under-invested venture, under-traded public markets, champions sold or scaled abroad — is not unique. Other middle powers faced the same gravity and built mechanisms to resist it: permanent fund-of-funds, contractual domestic reinvestment, retail tax wrappers, designated market-makers, mandatory savings pools. This is the comparator field, the working program toolkit, and the one model — Asian retail liquidity — that looks like magic but comes with an honest caveat.

— The program toolkit

Eight mechanisms that actually moved capital.

Strip away the labels and the toolkit is small. Two families dominate: supply-side leverage (state co-investment that pulls in private capital, then exits) and demand-side anchoring (a standing domestic bid — pensions, retail, insurers). The cleanest causal liquidity fix is neither — it is market-making plus mandated coverage.

KVIC Mother Fund · Korea
A permanent ₩10.9T state fund-of-funds (~3:1 private leverage). The Global Fund tranche requires ≥1× reinvestment back into Korea.
Lesson: a permanent FoF plus contractual domestic reinvestment keeps the value at home.
Yozma · Israel
1993 government VC: 40% public equity + a cheap buyout option for the private side. Fully privatized by 1998.
Lesson: seed a VC industry, build in the exit, then get out.
Initiative Tibi · France
A voluntary charter mobilizing insurer and institutional capital — €15B+ committed across two waves.
Lesson: a charter can mobilize insurer capital — but soft pledges leak.
Mansion House Accord · UK
Voluntary DC-pension pledge to domestic assets, backed by a statutory reserve power to mandate.
Lesson: voluntary pledges underdeliver — ~0.6% achieved against a 5% goal.
STAR Segment · Italy
Designated market-makers + mandated analyst coverage for qualifying mid-caps.
Lesson: coverage + market-making is the cleanest causal liquidity fix — spreads down 15–40%.
ISK Account · Sweden
A retail flat-tax investment wrapper now held by ~40% of the population.
Lesson: a simple retail tax wrapper builds a deep domestic equity base.
SBIC · United States
SBA debenture leverage at 2:1 over private capital — decades-old plumbing under US venture debt.
Lesson: cheap public leverage scales private deployment — but needs deep private capital first.
Superannuation · Australia
A$4.2T of mandatory retirement savings — a standing institutional bid that finances domestic markets.
Lesson: mandatory savings create a permanent domestic institutional demand base.

— The Asian liquidity model

Retail turnover drives the velocity outliers — but read it carefully.

The exchanges with the highest turnover velocity run on retail order flow: Korea ~64% and Taiwan ~54% of all turnover is retail. The retail→liquidity link is correlational, not proven causation. The cleanest causal lever is Italy's STAR — market-making plus mandated coverage. And Japan's NISA shows a retail wrapper can misfire: much of its flow went into foreign index funds, financing other countries' markets, not Japan's.
The lesson is not "import day-trading." It is: build a domestic demand base (retail wrapper or mandatory savings), give scaling companies a real growth board, and guarantee liquidity at the security level through market-making and coverage. A wrapper that funds foreign funds (NISA) buys you nothing at home.
— The read

The fixes exist. None is a single switch.

No comparator solved retention with one instrument. The ones that worked stacked a supply-side leverage program, a domestic demand anchor, and security-level liquidity support — and, where the value mattered most, made domestic reinvestment contractual rather than voluntary. That stack is the raw material for the recommendations.

05 — The Recommendations

Four levers to build, scale, and keep Canadian champions.

The levers follow directly from the diagnosis — under-investment at the scale-up stage, exit by acquisition or US listing, and thin liquidity — and from what works abroad. They lead with incentives, not mandates: the aim is to make backing, scaling, and keeping Canadian companies the rational private choice, not a compelled one.

scroll
— Lever ① · supply of growth capital

A Canadian Growth Capital Compact.

Tests hypotheses H-S1 / H-S2. The scale-up rounds Canada won't write are the binding supply constraint. The fix is an anchor fund-of-funds at market terms, with the matching, sweetener, and prudential carve-outs that crowd private money in — not a mandate that pushes it out.

Make the Growth VCCI permanent and scale it to C$3–5B
An anchor fund-of-funds deploying at market terms — the KVIC (Korea), Tesi (Finland) and NZ Growth Fund model. Four design bolts make it work:
0.3 : 1 government match
A co-investment match on institutional and insurer commitments — concessional enough to move capital, lean enough not to subsidise it.
Yozma-style sweetener
A buyout-at-cost + 5–7% / 5yr call option on the public stake (Israel's Yozma) — caps government upside, hands the winners to private GPs.
≥ 1× "reinvest in Canada" clause
A contractual condition that foreign VCs accessing the FoF redeploy at least 1× of drawn capital domestically — the Korea Global Fund mechanism.
OSFI LICAT carve-out
Cut the 40% capital factor toward 30% for qualifying domestic VC — mirroring Canada's own July-2025 infrastructure carve-out, freeing insurer balance sheets.
At VCCI's realised ~3:1 leverage, a C$3–5B FoF deploying ~C$1B/yr mobilises ~C$4B/yr total — about the size of the median scale-up gap.
Mandate verdict: against a hard pension mandate. Cumming calls it "a tax on pensioners"; the UK's Mansion House pledge under-delivered at 0.6% vs a 5% target; Korea's pure-structure approach crowds private capital out. Make domestic VC the rational choice instead.
Sources: VCCI program data; KVIC / Tesi / NZ Growth Fund design; Yozma & Korea Global Fund mechanisms; OSFI LICAT 2025 with the July-2025 infrastructure carve-out; Cumming (2026).
— Lever ② · demand-side capital

A Canadian Innovation Investment tax scheme.

Tests H-C1. A SEIS/EIS-style investor credit — roughly ~50% at seed, ~30% at growth — pulls private angel and early-growth money into qualifying Canadian companies. The UK's design is proven; copy it rather than reinvent it.

Copy the proven SEIS design
High-rate relief, a tight seed band, a risk-to-capital screen, a domestic-content requirement, and evaluation built in from day one. Avoid a broad patent box — it leaks to incumbents and rewards activity that would have happened anyway.
At ~C$0.43 per gross dollar mobilised, the scheme sits below the mining flow-through envelope Canada already funds every year — a costed, precedented ask, not a leap.

— Lever ③ · retention

Retention architecture.

Tests H-R2 / R3 / R4 / R6. Supplying capital isn't enough if champions are sold or relisted abroad the moment they scale. Four interlocking pieces keep pre-IPO companies — and their IP, HQ and hiring — anchored in Canada.

Pre-IPO liquidity venue
A PISCES-style private-secondary market plus a Canadian LTAF — letting founders and early employees realise liquidity without a sale or a US listing.
Retention conditionality
Attach Canadian-HQ / IP / hiring conditions to public grants — à la Quebec's CQCP and Israel's §19A IP-transfer levy.
Governance & control
Benefit-company form, facilitative dual-class with sunset-renewal, and steward-ownership structures. Necessary but not sufficient on their own.
Anti-take-private scoping
Scope measures against opportunistic take-privates — design-constrained by CUSMA and Canada's investment treaties, so calibrate carefully.
Governance tools alone don't keep a company home. Retention works only as an architecture — liquidity, conditionality, control and defence reinforcing one another.
Sources: UK PISCES framework; LTAF design; Quebec CQCP conditionality; Israel Innovation Authority §19A; benefit-company & dual-class governance literature; CUSMA investment-chapter constraints.
— Lever ④ · liquidity & coverage

Fix the trading and the research.

Tests H-S3 + H-C2. The diagnosis identified turnover velocity as the binding public-market constraint. The cleanest causal fixes are designated market-making, mandated coverage, and a retail demand base routed at domestic growth equity.

STAR-style market-maker + coverage
A Borsa Italiana STAR-style designated-market-maker and mandated analyst-coverage obligation for the TSXV / CSE — the cleanest causal liquidity fix in the evidence.
ISK-style retail account
A Swedish-ISK-style retail savings account designed to route to domestic growth equity — learning from Japan's NISA misfire, which leaked savings offshore.
Graduation escalator
Reinforce the TSXV → TSX graduation escalator so junior-board winners have a clear, low-friction path up to the senior board.
Sources: Borsa Italiana STAR segment rules; Sweden ISK account design; Japan NISA evaluation; TMX graduation framework. Causal claim per the WFE / OECD liquidity evidence — see Methodology.

— Key caveats

Where to be careful.

The honest reading of any synthesis is in its caveats. These are the limits a careful reader should hold in mind.

Market caps are point-in-time
TSX/TSXV as at Feb 2026; CSE end-2025. Several names have moved materially since the snapshot — treat them as a dated cross-section, not live quotes.
Private valuations are last-priced ⚠️
Figures for private champions are last-priced or press-reported rounds, flagged with ⚠️. They are not marks, audited values, or current.
Investor-domicile is indicative
"US-backed" / "Canadian-backed" tags reflect lead or notable investors, not full cap-table control or board math.
"AI company" is curated
The AI cohort is a curated list, not a bottom-up definition. Different definitions would shift counts at the margin.
The financing gap is a computation
Canada's VC/GDP vs. the comparator median and the US benchmark — a modelled estimate with a stated range, not an observed figure.
Korea KVIC figures are trade-press
The South Korea KVIC numbers are trade-press-sourced and should be read as indicative of scale, not audited.
Case studies are illustrative
The ~34 company studies illustrate mechanisms; they are not a statistical sample and don't support frequency claims.
CAD conversions are approximate
USD figures converted at ~US$1 = C$1.44. Cross-currency comparisons carry an FX margin.
