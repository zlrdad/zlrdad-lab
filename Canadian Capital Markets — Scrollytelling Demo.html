Ontario & Canadian Capital Markets

A system that is large by global standards yet structurally misaligned with the demands of a near-frontier innovation economy. Five findings that explain why.

The Landscape

The Centre of Canadian Finance

Ontario is the centre of gravity for Canadian capital markets. With a GDP of C$1.197 trillion in 2024 — roughly 38% of Canada's national output — Ontario anchors the country's financial infrastructure.

Toronto hosts the TSX and TSXV, Canada's Big Five banks, the majority of domestic VC and PE firms, and the OSC. The finance and insurance sector contributed C$93B in provincial GDP in 2024, growing 23.5% since 2019.

The Structural Problem

Built for Imitation, Not Innovation

Aghion and Howitt's distance-to-frontier framework draws a sharp distinction between institutional designs suited to near-frontier economies — easy entry, arm's-length finance, competitive markets — and those suited to catch-up economies, which benefit from concentrated incumbents and relationship banking.

As a near-frontier economy, Ontario's capital markets should be optimised for innovation. Yet several features of the Canadian system more closely resemble imitation-era institutional design.

Breznitz (2021) offers the sharpest diagnosis: Canada has everything academics say is needed to excel — top universities, high public R&D spending, and over 2,500 scientific papers per million inhabitants. Yet private-sector R&D has shrunk continuously to below 1% of GDP, the worst among OECD countries.

The root cause is that Canada has consistently confused innovation with invention — treating research policy as a substitute for genuine innovation policy.

Public Markets

A Giant Built on Concentration

The TSX is among the 10 largest stock exchanges globally by market capitalization. But size obscures a structural problem: the top 25 TSX operating companies hold 51.8% of total operating market cap. The top 100 hold 84.9%.

The S&P/TSX Composite is heavily weighted toward financials (~31%), energy (~18%), and materials (~12.5%), with information technology at only ~8% — a stark contrast to the NASDAQ-heavy US market.

The TSXV, originally designed as a stepping-stone to the TSX, has become overwhelmingly a mining-finance platform. Technology, Life Sciences, and Cleantech together represent 19% of operating issuers with just C$25.5B in combined market cap — 15.6% of the TSXV's operating total.

The median TSXV company has a market cap of C$15M. 70.9% are below C$50M. For growth-sector companies, the venture exchange offers limited liquidity and minimal institutional sponsorship.

IPO Markets

From 114 IPOs to 19 in Three Years

Canada's IPO market has contracted sharply. In 2022, 114 IPOs raised C$1.7 billion. By 2025, just 19 deals raised C$1.5 billion — fewer transactions but larger, driven by three TSX deals alone. The TSXV contributed 11 IPOs for a combined C$18 million.

The trend is clear: Canada's public markets are producing fewer but larger IPOs, concentrated on the TSX. The TSXV and CSE are generating minimal new listing activity for innovation companies.

Canadian GDP per capita has fallen from 98% of the US level in 2010 to just 61.3% in 2025 — a 37-percentage-point collapse in 15 years. The IPO market's contraction is both a symptom and a cause of this divergence: fewer liquidity events reduce founder wealth recycling and the capital available for the next generation of startups.

The IMF projects Canadian GDP growth at just 1.2% in 2025 — below the G7 average and well below the US at 2.0%.

The Financing Gap

Where Canadian Capital Runs Out

Canadian VC activity reached C$8.0B in primary deals in 2025 — healthy by historical standards. But who provides the capital tells a different story: foreign investors participated in 78% of total VC dollars, while Canadian-only rounds accounted for just 22% of dollars despite representing 61% of deal count.

Seed-stage investment declined 45% year-over-year in 2024, and early-stage (Series A/B) suffered the sharpest contraction in 2025 — down 28% by deal count. BDC Capital was the most active investor at both seed and early stage, underscoring government-backed capital's role in filling gaps that private capital is vacating.

The Series B gap is the most consequential structural problem. Companies that raise seed and Series A in Canada frequently need to raise Series B+ from US investors — who then often exert pressure to reincorporate in Delaware. Value leaks south before it can recycle into the Ontario ecosystem.

Canada has no investor-side tax incentive comparable to the UK's EIS/SEIS, which channelled ~£1.8B into early-stage companies in 2023–24. The absence of demand-side incentives compounds the structural supply gap at every stage.
